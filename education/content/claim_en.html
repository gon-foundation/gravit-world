How to check a claim

"Scientists have proved", "everybody knows", "100 % safe". Every day we hear claims. This lesson teaches pupils to give each claim a status instead of simply believing it or not.

Learning goals

  • Pupils tell apart a fact, an opinion and a claim without proof.
  • Pupils ask the four checking questions.
  • Pupils give a claim one of three statuses.

Four checking questions

1. Who says it?

A person, an organisation, an advert? What do they gain if you believe it?

2. What proves it?

Is there data, a measurement, a document? Or only words?

3. Can I check it myself?

Count, measure, find the original source, repeat the experiment.

4. Has someone else checked it?

Not the author's friend or company, but someone independent who got the same result.

Three statuses

Stated by the author

No proof yet, or only the author has checked it.

Independently confirmed

Someone else repeated the check and got the same result.

Disputed

Checks gave different results. New data is needed.

An opinion ("I like blue") cannot be checked and gets no status. That is not bad; it is simply a different kind of statement.

Exercise: sort the statements

Choose an answer for each statement. A hint appears after you choose.

2. This drink makes you smarter (advert).

Lesson plan for teachers

  1. 5 min. Ask the class: which claim did you hear this week and believe?
  2. 10 min. The four checking questions and the three statuses. Write them on the board.
  3. 10 min. The exercise with the whole class.
  4. 15 min. Groups of three: each invents a claim and a way to check it in the classroom (count, measure, find the source). One group checks another group's claim.
  5. 5 min. Summary: "Do not believe and do not argue: check. First the proof, then the status."

Homework

At home, find one claim (on a package, in an advert, in the news) and ask it the four questions. Write down which status you gave it and why.
